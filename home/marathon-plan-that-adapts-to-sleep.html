Does one bad night ruin a run?

Usually not. A large review of sleep-loss studies found the effects on exercise are mixed: some maximal efforts hold up after a short night, while studies in athletes report drops in sport-specific performance, and sleep loss reliably hurts mood, reaction time and accuracy. One rough night before an easy run is rarely a reason to skip it. Several short nights in a row, plus heavy legs and high stress, is a different story.

Why adjust the hard days, not the easy ones

In a 2016 study of 40 recreational runners, one group followed a fixed plan and the other did its harder sessions only on days when morning heart rate variability was in its normal range. The HRV-guided group did fewer moderate and hard sessions, yet improved its 3000 m time by about 2% (the fixed-plan group's change was not significant). Both groups improved VO2max. The lesson: timing your hard sessions to the days you're recovered can matter more than hitting every one on the calendar. That's why the advice above trims or moves workouts and long runs first, and keeps easy running easy.

How much sleep do runners need?

A 2021 expert consensus on sleep in athletes notes that many athletes sleep short or broken, and recommends an individual target over one fixed number: how much you need to feel rested, not a rule like "exactly 8". If you're under 5 hours, this check treats the day as a recovery day no matter how the other answers look.

How the score works

  • Out of 100: sleep hours up to 8 (30 points), sleep quality (20), fresh legs (20), energy (20), low stress (10).
  • Watch data: HRV clearly below your 2-week normal takes off 8, resting HR 5+ bpm high takes off 6, a sleep score under 60 takes off 5.
  • 70 or more: ready to train. 50 to 69: go easier. Under 50, or under 5 hours of sleep: recovery day.
