Can't run 3 miles yet?

Start one step earlier. In Mileset, pick Get fitter and healthier and choose Running as your cardio. If you're not active right now or move 1–2 days a week, your runs start as walk-runs: short runs with walking breaks, and the runs get longer every couple of weeks. Once a few miles feel easy, switch your goal to a 5K.

How the 8 weeks build

  • Weeks 1–3: base. Mostly easy running, plus one light session a week (hill sprints, a progression run or a fartlek) to build form before the harder work.
  • Weeks 4–6: build. One faster session a week: 800s, mile repeats or 1K repeats. Week 4 is lighter, with a shorter long run, so your body catches up. The long run never grows by more than 2 miles from one week to the next.
  • Week 7: peak. The most running of the plan, still with just one fast session.
  • Week 8: race week. Short and easy, a few quick 400s at goal pace to stay sharp, then the 5K.

What the efforts mean

  • Easy: you can talk in full sentences. Most of your running should feel like this.
  • Threshold: comfortably hard. You could say a few words, not hold a conversation.
  • Interval and fast: hard but controlled, about the effort you could hold for a 5K or a bit quicker. Jog the recoveries.
  • Goal pace: the pace you want to run on race day.

Lifting? Strength goes on run days so your easy days stay easy, never on the long run day, and turns light in the lighter week and race week.

Why build up slowly

Big jumps are where new runners get hurt. In a study of 874 novice runners followed for a year with GPS watches, those who increased their weekly distance by more than 30% had more distance-related injuries than those who increased it by less than 10%. The result was not statistically significant and the authors call it exploratory, but it points the same way as a larger 2025 study of 5,205 runners: single runs more than 10% longer than your longest run of the previous 30 days came with a higher injury rate.
